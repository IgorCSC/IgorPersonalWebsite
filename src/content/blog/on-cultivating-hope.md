There is a famous passage in The Lord of The Rings that has been popping up in mind recently. Confronting his dire situation, a quest where only a fool would hope for success, Frodo says to Gandalf:

> “I wish it need not have happened in my time," said Frodo.  
> 
> "So do I," said Gandalf, "and so do all who live to see such times. But that is not for them to decide. All we have to decide is what to do with the time that is given us.

This is not a new feeling to us, humans. One would prefer not to live in the time of the plague, or being raided by the Mongol army, or experience in first hand the arrival of the Spanish Conquistadors, or starve in Bengal or Holodomor. The list is vast. Most of the time, our situation was very precarious. Sometimes, I think about my great-grandparents, who crossed continents by boat to flee poverty and saw half of their children die in their early twenties from (now preventable) infectious diseases such as Typhoid fever and Tuberculosis. I believe they also didn't want to live through that. 

Right now, we still face a bunch of problems. There still is cancer and dementia; war is coming back; famine and genocide are no strangers, but, mostly, things are getting better. This is the mandatory moment in which Steven Pinker pops up and interjects -- _actually, you should be grateful! You live in the best of times!_ There is, however, a bitter aftertaste. The impression that this may be it, that we can't possibly keep on going with some trends that fly by our tired eyes, that we are headed to disaster even if we are somewhat cozy right now. Some people wish that they lived their parents or grandparents lives. For some time, I reflected on this topic by asking myself _is this picture correct? Is the world getting better or getting worse? Or is it a secret third thing? Are we headed to disaster?_ Lately, I've come to change the perspective a bit. My answer to those questions is probably a boring _well, it's complicated_, but the questions I pose myself mostly nowadays are different: how should one live with such uncertainty? and is having hope worth it? And I think that, yes, no matter what, it is worth it.

One of the reasons behind The Lord of the Rings' success is its narrative arc that portrays hope as a fundamental value conducting the heroes to the fulfillment of their goals. Gandalf, who architects the plan, is questioned by all sides as a madman. It seems insane to put so much hope in such a fragile, ignorant, and weak ring-bearer. Gandalf himself quickly succumbs in Moria, leaving Frodo and Sam alone to carry out their mission. And, against all odds, by a combination of sheer luck, endurance, friendship, ingenuity, predestination, and plain goodness, they triumph. Their victory wouldn't exist if they had simply abandoned their arms and rationally coped with their fates, a temptation that arises more than once during their ordeal. Despite all things, they kept hope up to the very end, even after crossing the Black Gate.

The Lord of the Rings is just a story, though. Of course, they win at the end. It's not even high literature, one may say. And yet, there seems to be wisdom buried in this narrative. Recently, I've read Viktor Frankl's most known book, Man's Search for Meaning. Frankl, a Jewish psychiatrist, developed a kind of talk therapy related to classical psychoanalysis called logotherapy that focuses on the meaning in individual human lives. Part of his theory grew from his experiences as an inmate in the concentration camp of Auschwitz-Birkenau. Being a prisoner in such a camp was one of such destinies that one would prefer not to live, to say the least. At arrival, Frankl recounts that he and the other prisoners were separated into two groups by the criteria of work fitness, as judged by a quick glance from a camp doctor. At the time, they did not know that the unfit half would burn in the ovens in that very day.

Pain, hunger and cold were constant companions. The starved, frost bitten inmates did exhausting work while being humiliated by the guards, having to keep in mind that at the faintest sign of weakness they could be simply executed. Frankl recounts that the situation was so bad that even suicide lost its meaning. It was simply the default outcome: "There was little point in committing suicide, since, for the average inmate, life expectation, calculating objectively and counting all likely chances, was very poor. He could not with any assurance expect to be among the small percentage of men who survived all the selections." Objectively, survival odds were very low. At one point, speaking to a group of prisoners, Frankl estimates his own chances as one in twenty, or 5%, but concludes by saying that "But I also told them that, in spite of this, I had no intention of losing hope and giving up. For no man knew what the future would bring, much less the next hour."

One of Frankl's fundamental insights is that having a reason to live, which includes hope and faith in the future, is a core component of keeping sanity, enduring suffering, and, surprisingly, surviving hardships. He quotes from Nietzsche -- “_He who has a why to live for can bear with almost any how_". There is a defiant, courageous aspect to maintaining hope even when there are no reasons to expect favorable outcomes. Even when the rational posture would not certify it. And there is also a self-fulfilling aspect to it. While man cannot simply hope the problems away -- that is not his message at all! --, the odds may slightly favor those who actively cultivate hope. At least, they are ready to grab any ladder that is thrown at them by providence, as in [the parable of the drowning man](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parable_of_the_drowning_man).

The reverse was of course also true. Frankl recounts watching people simply succumb and die when they lost hope, testifying to the interconnected nature of mind and body. He goes on to saying that 

> The prisoner who had lost faith in the future—his future—was doomed. With his loss of belief in the future, he also lost his spiritual hold; he let himself decline and became subject to mental and physical decay.

and

> Woe to him who saw no more sense in his life, no aim, no purpose, and therefore no point in carrying on. He was soon lost. The typical reply with which such a man rejected all encouraging arguments was, “I have nothing to expect from life any more.” What sort of answer can one give to that?

My default opinion on such matters was, until recently, negative. One should accept the raw, crude reality, and having hope is simply self-delusion, I thought. I've come to revise this position. While I'll admit that a central component of this change was a series of personal, non-intellectual, events in my life, I do think that there is an articulate, reasonable and defensible reading of this change. I also point at this aspirational nature -- cultivating hope is not a natural, given state, at least to me. Sometimes, it'll not work, but there is reason to aim for it regardless.

The first blind spot of my earlier, cynical vision is believing hope to be necessarily tied to delusion. But hope does not need to be a false belief that everything will turn out, no matter what. Hope is -- to quote Nick Cave, who I'll discuss once more a bit later -- "optimism with a broken heart". It is not believing that the best outcome always prevail, but striving towards it, imagining it, and, more important, do not rejoicing in self-congratulatory defeatism.

I had already drafted some ideas to this text when a friend shared a video featuring Nick Cave reading a letter he wrote to a fan on the topic. His text not only aligned with some topics I intended to cover, but also expanded them and, frankly, exposed them more eloquently than I could ever. The mandatory background is that Cave lost two sons, something that is painful even to entertain imaginatively. 

[The brief letter](https://www.theredhandfiles.com/do-you-still-believe-in-us/) -- which I think you should read: it is only three paragraphs long! -- is an answer to a fan who is becoming more and more cynical and is afraid that he'll pass the attitude to his son. Cave points to some characteristics of both cynicism and hope. He notes that cynicism is not neutral, and, although asks almost nothing from us, is highly infectious and unbelievable destructive. The cynic perspective often portrays itself as sober and realistic, a cold look at the matter of facts, but is incredibly self-complacent and comfortable. By recognizing defeat earlier, one is entirely liberated from any kind of responsibility and turns oneself into a mere spectator. Cynicism is, then, not an act of courage facing delusion, but often a cowardice demanding comfort. That our problems are many and difficult is hardly overstated. Some of them may well be beyond our reach, and yet, there are so many things to accomplish, so many different-shaped problems, and so much work to do that the option of standing with one's arms crossed while everything crumbles is absurd. I know some people that argue that, because problems are 'structural', meaning that they are too big to be tackled by the action of individuals or small groups, no action to alleviate them should be taken. I disagree vehemently with this position. If there is widespread hunger, feeding one hungry human being is infinitely better than doing nothing, or vaguely gesturing toward an out-of-reach imagined utopia. This is true even for small acts of caring. Those acts do not only improve their recipient lives and the world in general, but they are also incredibly transformative to those on the giving end.
