Despite the post's title, this is not my first Kurosawa movie. I've already watched Dreams, Kagemusha, Rashomon, and Ikiru, each a long time ago. This, however, is part of a little project with high chances of failure that I'm starting. The idea is to watch a couple of essential Kurosawa movies. I've been intending to do this for a long time and two things pushed me to start. My first year of parenting was really incompatible with film watching. We want to avoid exposing the little one to screens and when he goes to sleep we are usually tired and don't want to risk waking him up by turning on the TV right next to his bedroom. This made movies really scarce and created the an urge to choose very carefully what to watch. I am not really a cinephile in any way and my general cinema culture is lacking. My idea was to lessen this at least a bit by becoming acquainted with some classics. I already knew and liked some Kurosawa movies and the video essay The Humanistic Cinema of Akira Kurosawa was the last piece of incentive I needed to try it out.

I chose Yojimbo as the first one because it is both an older movie; not so long, with 1h50m; and said to be an easy one. Therefore, a good place to start. Although it is in black and white, it is by no means the stereotypical cult movie. It's a samurai story and an action movie -- although one from the early sixties, so don't expect a modern pacing. Overall, I was surprised with how watchable the movie is. It doesn't really drag, has a simple but interesting plot and group of characters and some thrilling scenes. The acting may cause some strangeness to the novice viewer: it's old Japanese cinema and Kurosawa. The actors do not aim for some kind of realism, but rather seek expressiveness through exaggerated movements and a characteristic display of facial emotions. The fight scenes are quick and choreographed, but not in over-the-top Chinese wuxia kung-fu movies. The swords do not seem to really touch the fighters and the blood always appears only after the fact. The swordplay, with rapid katana blows leaving a trail of stunned bodies behind surely passed on to anime -- more than once I recalled my youth days of watching Rurouni Kenshin.

The most salient part to someone used to modern cinema, however, is how good pretty much every scene looks. To borrow the name of a famous and defunct Youtube channel: every frame is a painting. It is strange, because it seems that as a culture we accepted that cinema, although heavily reliant on visuals, does not need to be visually stunning. There is also a great deal of visual storytelling. The story is partly told by dialogue, but a lot of the characters, including our samurai hero, are not very talkative, and camera angles, movement, and composition do a lot of the narrative heavy lifting. Sometimes, the camera is almost a character joining the hero. The viewer joins the hero peeking through a window to watch a government inspector being bribed, as if they were another person in the room.
Mifune slowing walking for a final showdown. Why doesn't every movie look like this?

The plot is straightforward. A mysterious ronin (Toshiro Mifune) walks into a ravished town. He learns that there is a conflict between two mafia-like guys, who are having a feud for controlling the territory and destroying any semblance of normality that there once was in the village. A man that should be the face of justice just watch from afar, helping the mobsters to recruit more fighters in exchange for some scraps of silver, and the only one making a decent amount of money is the coffin maker. The ronin embodies this western-like figure of a tough guy that deep down a good heart. He joins an innkeeper in a reckless plan to make each of the mobsters to eliminate the other, therefore freeing the city of the conflict.

During the course of the movie, we are confronted with a series of symptoms of a dysfunctional and deeply corrupted society. Justice is blind and a government inspector is bribed away by tea, sake, and prostitutes; violence is the only currency that yields effective power; regular people are subjected to the arbitrariness of the mob and exposed to the ill effects of predatory gambling; women are traded and used as objects. The ronin clearly opposes this state of affairs, although he has no theory of how things should be. He also speaks through the same language of violence, a language in which he is more versed than his adversaries. The tension between the old samurai code of conduct, the Bushido way of the warrior, and the new warfare is exemplified by Unosuke (Tatsuya Nakagai), a mobster that comes back from a trip bearing a pistol, a gun that is portrayed as treacherous, yet powerful, in contrast with the nobility of the samurai sword, with its clean and precise movements.

Overall, I really enjoyed the movie and would recommend without further thought. For the next one, I'm thinking either of continuing the ronin story with the direct sequel Sanjuro (1962), or moving on to a completely different setting. Some options are I Live in Fear; High and Low; Red Beard or Dersu Uzala. I don't know when I'll have the time and disposition, so I'll leave it to whatever I feel at the moment.

Episode 42 of the first season, Showdown at Dark City, is a direct homage to the movie. I'm puzzled by the inner workings of my memory.
