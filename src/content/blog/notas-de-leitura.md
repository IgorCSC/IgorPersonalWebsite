I was a child when the idea of death first struck me. Humans are mortal, and I was just one of them -- the syllogism followed naturally. But how could this be?, I asked myself. I was baffled. After all, I was not some human, I was the human. Without me, there would be no universe, as the universe would have no audience. I could not imagine what not existing would be like. At some point, I reasoned that if my heart stopped, I'd still have some seconds of life (or so I thought). At that time, I started compulsively checking my heartbeat to investigate whether those were the last moments of my subjective experience.

In any case, I kept struggling intellectually with the idea, trying to come with a representation of what death would be like. One of the images that I conjured, the one that resonated more with me, was the following: suddenly, I opened my eyes into consciousness. I was falling into a deep well. The walls were smooth and with no irregularities. I realized that there was a bottom somewhere, even though I could not readily see it. I needed to stop the fall! I tried changing the position or grabbing the wall, to no avail. It was simply not possible to defeat gravity. The most I could accomplish was to scratch the walls, leaving faint markings on the surface. The marks could be seen by another falling person in principle. A metaphor of achieving immortality through work -- really unfulfilling. Whenever I pictured this scenario, I would get chills down my spine and feel a deep existential dread.

I always thought that this depiction of life was idiosyncratic and original, until I read this short snippet by Franz Kafka.

    "Alas", said the mouse, "the whole world is growing smaller every day. At the beginning it was so big that I was afraid, I kept running and running, and I was glad when I saw walls far away to the right and left, but these long walls have narrowed so quickly that I am in the last chamber already, and there in the corner stands the trap that I am running into." "You only need to change your direction," said the cat, and ate it up.

The mouse was not falling as in my dream, but somehow he had no option but to run forward in his tunnel. After all, there was a cat just behind. Kafka's short fable adds a new layer to the metaphor: as time goes by, the tunnel gets narrower. This detail adds both the idea of the pruning of possibilities as life passes by, as well as the progressive deterioration of one's overall capabilities. There is no way out and the cat awaits us all in the end.

A few days ago I found yet another very similar visual description of life and death, which makes me think that my metaphor is not that original and may be connected to more fundamental aspects of human phenomenology. The passage is in Reasons and Persons, the major book by the British philosopher Derek Parfit. It starts like this:

    When I believed that my existence was such a further fact, I seemed imprisoned in myself. My life seemed like a glass tunnel, through which I was moving faster every year, and at the end of which there was darkness.

Unlike my younger self or the mouse in Kafka's fable, the late Parfit allegedly managed to overcome the dread stemming from this picture by transforming his own concept of personal identity. This transformation, according to Parfit, dissolved the potency of Death:

    When I changed my view, the walls of my glass tunnel disappeared. I now live in the open air. There is still a difference between my life and the lives of other people. But the difference is less. Other people are closer. I am less concerned about the rest of my own life, and more concerned about the lives of others. (...) My death will break the more direct relations between my present experiences and future experiences, but it will not break various other relations. This is all there is to the fact that there will be no one living who will be me. Now that I have seen this, my death seems to me less bad.

If Parfit is correct, the solution to life's oldest problem is not stopping or reversing gravity, nor finding a way out of the maze, but simply recognizing that there are no walls.
